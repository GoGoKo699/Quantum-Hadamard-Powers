# Benchmarks and related work

## Coherent square on a clean state-preparation oracle

The algorithm prepares an approximation to the normalized coordinatewise square of a clean source in $`O_\epsilon(N^{1/3})`$ calls for any amplitudes. Here $`\epsilon`$ is a fixed trace-error and failure budget. The hard budget includes all attempted calls, including both directions of the source. Using a fixed fallback state on failure converts the procedure into an always-output algorithm. Allocate part of the error budget to that branch by convexity of trace distance.

Proposition 8.2 of Bhateja, Esmaili and Mantri gives $`\Omega(N^{1/3})`$ calls for preparing the normalized Hadamard square of a Haar-random state at any fixed mean trace error below $`1/12`$. The bound allows the canonical swap oracle or the Haar-unitary interface, where the source prepares the input from a known basis state. The lower bound applies to the worst-case hard query budget of an algorithm with the pointwise guarantee above. This gives a matching exponent for the square. Higher powers have constructive upper bounds here. [Bhateja, Esmaili and Mantri, §8.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06721v1#S8.SS2)

## Squared-law sampling with the same evaluator on both sides

Let $`M`$ be even. A function $`g:[M]\to[M]`$ is promised to be one-to-one or exactly two-to-one on its image. The quantum evaluator XORs $`g(i)`$ into a target register. On the classical side the algorithm may choose any input $`i`$ and read $`g(i)`$, including when that choice depends on previous answers. Each evaluation in either model costs one query.

Draw a fair tag $`b`$ and an independent uniform input $`i`$. The sampler outputs $`(0,i)`$ on tag zero and $`(1,g(i))`$ on tag one. Quantum preparation keeps $`i`$ as an auxiliary register and implements this sampler reversibly. For example, compute $`g(i)`$ into work space, conditionally write it to the outcome on tag one, then uncompute $`g(i)`$. The source and its adjoint each cost at most two evaluator calls. The retained input is allowed by the measured-law form of the algorithm.

The sum of squared probabilities on tag zero is always $`1/(4M)`$. On tag one it is $`1/(4M)`$ in the one-to-one case and $`1/(2M)`$ in the two-to-one case. Consequently, the squared law puts probability $`1/2`$ on tag zero in the first case and $`1/3`$ in the second. For example, with $`M=4`$ the one-to-one distribution has eight outcomes of weight $`1/8`$. The two-to-one distribution has four tag-zero outcomes of weight $`1/8`$ and two tag-one outcomes of weight $`1/4`$. Their squared norms are $`1/8`$ and $`3/16`$.

An output within total variation $`1/24`$ of the target changes each tag probability by at most $`1/24`$. The tag-zero frequencies in the two cases remain separated by at least $`1/12`$, so a constant number of fresh samples distinguishes the cases with bounded error. The TV budget includes fallback on failed quantum or classical attempts. Kutin's collision bound then gives $`\Omega(M^{1/3})`$ quantum evaluator calls. His modular-addition evaluator simulates each XOR evaluation with two calls by computing into scratch and uncomputing after the XOR. Known modular negations turn a forward modular-addition call into its inverse. The spectral algorithm gives $`O(M^{1/3})`$ in this family because the label alphabet has size $`2M`$. [Kutin, Theorem 1.1 and §2.1](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v001a002/v001a002.pdf)

For the classical lower bound, draw a uniform permutation in the one-to-one case. In the two-to-one case, draw a uniform perfect matching of the inputs, then assign different output labels to its pairs uniformly. Before the first repeated output on different inputs, the two transcripts can be coupled with the same fresh labels. After $`j`$ distinct inputs have been queried without a collision, their partners in the matching are uniformly placed among the remaining inputs. The probability that the next new query is such a partner is $`j/(M-j)`$. Thus the chance that a $`q`$-query transcript breaks the coupling is at most $`\sum_{j<q}j/(M-j)=O(q^2/M)`$ for $`q\leq M/2`$. Repeated queries reveal only answers already known. Bounded-error distinction, and hence the sampling task, requires $`\Omega(\sqrt M)`$ classical evaluations.

There is a matching classical upper bound for this family. Generate $`t=\lfloor\sqrt M\rfloor`$ independent tagged samples for an attempt, with $`t=2`$ if this is smaller. Draw the same uniform rank and test the same predicate used in the coherent procedure. For $`r=2`$ the good probability is $`\binom{t}{2}S_2 Q_2(t-2,p)/4`$. The collision moment satisfies $`1/(2M)\leq S_2\leq 3/(4M)`$, and the union bound gives $`Q_2(t-2,p)\geq 1-\binom{t-2}{2}S_2`$. Thus one attempt succeeds with constant probability and returns the exact squared law when it succeeds. A constant number of attempts attains fixed small TV error with $`O(\sqrt M)`$ classical evaluations. This establishes a worst-case evaluator separation for sampling. The coherent-state lower bound above supplies the separate benchmark for clean quantum output.

## A rare-label stress test

Hide a label $`h`$ uniformly among $`N`$ labels and set $`p_h=2/\sqrt{N}`$ with the remaining mass uniform. The target gives $`h`$ probability tending to $`4/5`$, but $`m`$ ordinary draws reveal it with probability at most $`2m/\sqrt N`$. Conditional on never seeing it, the observed transcript is symmetric among unseen labels, so a guess for $`h`$ adds probability at most $`1/(N-m)`$. This illustrates why a table of $`O(N^{1/3})`$ measured labels cannot supply the rare label at the frequency required by the squared law.

The same lower bound holds for a classical device that uses the canonical swap oracle on basis inputs and reads the output in that basis. This oracle exchanges a known marker $`|\bot\rangle`$ and $`|\psi\rangle`$ and acts identically on their orthogonal complement. The marker input gives one draw from $`p`$. On input $`i`$ the output probabilities are $`p_i`$ for $`\bot`$, $`p_i p_j`$ for $`j\neq i`$, and $`(1-p_i)^2`$ for $`i`$. To simulate that call, take two independent draws $`X_1,X_2`$. Output $`\bot`$ if $`X_1=i`$, output $`X_1`$ if $`X_1\neq i`$ and $`X_2=i`$, and otherwise output $`i`$. This also simulates adaptive basis queries at a factor of at most two ordinary samples. The quantum algorithm uses the same swap oracle as a source by using $`|\bot\rangle`$ as its known basis input. [Bhateja, Esmaili and Mantri, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06721v1#S3.SS1)

| [Bhateja, Esmaili and Mantri, Theorems 6.6 and 8.1 and Proposition 8.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06721v1) | Their measured sampler uses $`O(N^{1/3})`$ queries for the squared law, with accuracy measured in ensemble-average TV. The present algorithm handles arbitrary complex amplitudes of a single input raised to a fixed power, with a per-input guarantee. |

Their importance-sampling bound for real $`P(x)=x^r`$ contains the normalization factor $`\sqrt{S_r}`$ and $`\max_{|x|\leq 1}|P(x)/x|=1`$. Their separate complexity improvements for other functions depend on the relevant normalization. [Guo, Mitarai and Fujii](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.10764) study amplitude transforms of separate real and imaginary parts using QSVT and controlled source calls. |
| [Magniez, Nayak, Roland and Santha](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0608026) and [Krovi, Magniez, Ozols and Roland](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.2419) | Provide quantum-walk precedents for spectral filtering and reweighting marked mass. Here local source projectors and a marked-rank predicate implement a tilted operator directly. Its wanted component is the exact coherent extraction direction. |
